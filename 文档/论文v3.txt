How Reproducible Are Historical Arguments? A Controlled Experiment with Large Language Models
Ning Zhou; Chenxi Zhao; Siwei Zhang; Shiting Guan; Tongen Jin ; Xi Gao
Abstract
Keywords
Introduction
In the seventeenth century, Robert Boyle pioneered the use of virtual witnessing in air-pump experiments. By providing detailed experimental records, he enabled others to reproduce his results [P]. The principle of reproducibility embodied in this practice became a cornerstone of modern science as scientific communities developed [P]. Large language models (LLMs) have introduced new approaches to reproducibility in the natural and social sciences. For example, silicon replication has been used to simulate and predict responses in sociological surveys and experiments [A, C]. Although the humanistic value of such studies remains contested [F, G], they point to new methodological possibilities for scientific reproducibility.
Historically, discussions of reproducibility have focused primarily on the natural and social sciences [J], with comparatively little attention paid to the humanities. Three common arguments have been used to explain this difference: the uniqueness of objects of study in the humanities, the diversity of its methods, and the normative significance of its interpretations [B]. Yet even if, as Peels argues, humanities research is reproducible in principle [B, I], its practical feasibility and the conditions under which reproduction succeeds remain open empirical questions.
In principle, multiple scholars with relevant expertise could independently analyse the same set of materials, allowing their findings to be compared. In practice, however, recruiting a statistically sufficient number of researchers to analyse the same question would be prohibitively expensive. Moreover, key variables, including researchers' educational backgrounds, training, motivations and funding sources, are difficult to control. Even when researchers reach the same conclusions, it may still be difficult to establish whether a study has been successfully reproduced. Furthermore, as Peels's discussion illustrates, reproducibility in the humanities has generally focused on conclusions rather than on the reasoning processes that lead to them. One reason is that extracting the full argumentative structure of a scholarly article is itself costly and methodologically difficult, whether performed by students or by experts [K, L].
However, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) provides new technical conditions for testing the reproducibility of historical argumentation and, through it, a tractable empirical case for studying reproducibility in the humanities. First, LLMs substantially reduce the cost of generating text with a logically coherent form, making large-scale and repeated analyses more feasible. Second, by fixing the model and its sampling parameters, researchers can reduce variation attributable to differences between human authors, including their educational backgrounds and knowledge. Although individual outputs may vary, the model's pretrained parameters generally remain unchanged during an experiment. Third, carefully designed prompts allow researchers to approximate selected conditions under which an original argument was produced and to operationalize epistemological principles that were previously difficult to control or examine, making quantitative analysis possible. Finally, purpose-built pipelines enable LLMs to extract the argumentative structure of scholarly texts with relative consistency. Although these extracted structures may not correspond to the texts' underlying argumentative structures in an ontological sense, they provide operational representations that allow human- and model-derived structures to be observed and compared.
Here, we present a controlled experiment using LLMs to investigate the reproducibility of historical argumentation. We use historical scholarship as a controlled empirical case through which to study a broader metascientific question: under what conditions can evidence-based arguments in the humanities be reproduced? The design varies source-context length and prompt condition within each article, with article and source-material topic providing three thematic strata. The six prompt-by-context conditions will be evaluated with two generation models, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-5.6 Sol. Reasoning effort will be held constant at medium in both models. We develop two outcome measures, the claim convergence index (CCI) and the edge convergence index (ECI), to assess convergence between model-generated and original human-authored arguments at the levels of argumentative claims and their relations.
Given the lack of an established operational definition for the reproducibility of complex historical arguments, we use CCI and ECI as exploratory proxies for their structural reproducibility. CCI measures convergence in the propositions and judgments represented in the two arguments, whereas ECI measures convergence in the logical relations between propositions and between propositions and source materials. Together, these measures extend the assessment beyond conclusions alone to encompass the structure of argumentation and its connections to evidence. In particular, ECI allows us to examine how source materials are linked to propositions and how these relations are distributed within an argument.
The argument-tree representation also makes the comparison more auditable. A direct LLM judgment of whether two complete papers are similar would collapse many distinct decisions into a single score, making it difficult to identify which propositions, evidentiary links, or inferential relations produced that score; decomposed evaluation has likewise been proposed as an interpretable alternative to undifferentiated pointwise LLM scoring [O]. Previous research on argument mining has shown the value of representing claims, premises, and their relations explicitly [K, L], while work on argument similarity has found that graph-based representations can provide interpretable evidence for similarity judgments [M]. Our encoding converts the comparison into inspectable node-level and edge-level decisions: every matched claim can be traced to the corresponding claim texts, and every matched edge can be traced either to an explicit claim-to-claim relation or to a claim-to-source relation. This design does not eliminate possible errors or biases in LLM judgments, but it makes the inputs, intermediate alignments, and resulting scores available for human review. This qualification is important because LLM judges have been shown to exhibit systematic biases, including sensitivity to candidate position [N].
Based on our experimental design and research objectives, we propose three hypotheses:
H1: CCI and ECI decrease as the amount of source material increases.
H2: CCI and ECI increase as the research question shifts towards an evidence-driven approach (lun cong shi chu, “arguments derived from historical evidence”).
H3: CCI and ECI vary across article and source-material topics.
This study makes four contributions. First, it introduces a controlled experimental approach to investigating the boundaries of reproducibility in historical argumentation. Second, it establishes a framework that could be adapted, with discipline-specific validation, to other evidence-based humanities fields by incorporating appropriate databases, argumentative representations and prompts. Third, it provides an openly accessible database and codebase to facilitate the inspection and reproduction of our analyses. Finally, it offers an empirical baseline for historical scholarship and a starting point for comparative investigations of reproducibility across the humanities.

Discussion
Methods
Our experiment comprised six main stages: data collection; data processing and cleaning; dataset construction; extraction of argument trees from human-authored historical papers; generation and processing of model-authored papers; and data analysis and statistical evaluation.
1. Data Collection
We first compared major historical journals in english, including The American Historical Review and Past & Present, with leading Chinese historical journals, such as Historical Research (《历史研究》). We ultimately selected 30 articles published in Song History Review (《宋史研究论丛》), a CSSCI-indexed, peer-reviewed Chinese journal specializing in Song dynasty history, comprising ten articles each in political, economic, and intellectual history.
This selection was guided by four methodological considerations.
First, citation practices. Chinese historical scholarship typically makes explicit and direct references to historical sources, whereas references in English-language historical scholarship more frequently involve indirect citation practices. Explicit citations allow us to trace the relationship between historical evidence and the surrounding argumentative context with less reliance on researcher interpretation, thereby facilitating the operationalization of our independent variable.
Second, evidentiary constraints. As a traditional history journal, Song History Review makes relatively limited use of social science methods and adheres more closely to the source-based argumentative practices characteristic of traditional historical scholarship.
Third, the scale and accessibility of historical sources. The source base for Song dynasty history is sufficiently large to support variation across articles while remaining operationally manageable for the present computational design. Moreover, the digitization and OCR processing of a substantial portion of these sources improve their accessibility and suitability for automated processing.
Fourth, the control of thematic variation. The journal's classification of articles into social, economic, and intellectual history provides an existing thematic framework, reducing the degree of subjective judgment required for topic assignment. Restricting the sample to a single journal also helps maintain a degree of consistency in publication conventions and disciplinary practices across the selected articles.
2. Data Processing and Cleaning
We converted the PDF files into JSON-formatted documents while preserving their footnotes. We then used regular-expression-based scripts to extract the footnotes and assigned a unique identifier to each source citation in each article. For example, A01M1 denotes the first source citation (M1) in the first article (A01) of the corresponding thematic category A（economy）.
We subsequently cleaned the extracted data by removing indirect citations and references to modern secondary literature, retaining only citations that directly referred to historical sources. We also removed non-substantive front matter, including author information and abstracts, from the article texts. These procedures were intended to ensure that the resulting dataset consistently represented the relationship between historical evidence and scholarly argument.
3. Dataset Construction
Building on the cleaned citation data, we used open-access databases to retrieve approximately 400 Chinese characters of surrounding context for each retained historical source citation. We then constructed three datasets at different contextual scales for each article: S (short), M (medium), and L (long).
All three datasets retained the same set of valid historical source citations from the original article. They differed only in the amount of textual context associated with each citation: S contained the cited sentence itself, M included the cited sentence and approximately 100 Chinese characters of surrounding text on either side, and L included the cited sentence and approximately 200 Chinese characters on either side. This design allowed us to examine how the amount of contextual information available to the model affected its reconstruction of historical arguments while holding the underlying set of source citations constant.
4. Extraction of Logical Trees from Human-Authored Papers
Following preliminary tool validation, we extracted one hierarchical argument tree from each complete article. The extraction unit was the full paper rather than an isolated paragraph. This choice allowed the model to assign a paragraph-level claim only after observing the article's conclusion, section structure, and the relations among all paragraphs. The procedure comprised the following steps.
First, we prepared a structured input for each article. The cleaned text was divided into paragraphs and assigned stable paragraph identifiers (P1, P2, and so forth). Section headings and their spans were recorded explicitly. Front matter and modern secondary references had already been removed during data cleaning, while the identifiers of retained historical sources (M) were kept unchanged. Where an article did not contain an explicit conclusion heading, a manually checked conclusion-span file specified the paragraphs from which the overall conclusion could be reconstructed. Each article was required to have exactly one article-level conclusion.
Second, we constructed the non-evidentiary skeleton of the tree. A denoted the article's single overall conclusion. B nodes denoted section-level claims and were anchored to the article's heading hierarchy and paragraph spans. Their identifiers and positions were therefore determined before claim extraction. C nodes denoted paragraph-anchored argumentative propositions: the smallest claim units represented in the tree. A C node was not an original sentence or an entire paragraph. Rather, it was a normalized declarative sentence that paraphrased or synthesized the paragraph's principal interpretive, evaluative, or causal judgment. The paragraph supplied the node's provenance and constrained its evidence binding, while the proposition expressed by the normalized sentence was the unit used in semantic alignment. The extraction prompt allowed at most one C node for each body paragraph and omitted paragraphs containing only quotations, historical records, legal provisions, prices, dates, other raw observations, or transitions. Because one C node could synthesize a multi-sentence paragraph, we treated it as an operational claim unit rather than as an atomic proposition in the formal-logical sense. Section headings served as structural anchors; they were not treated by themselves as evidence of a logical support relation.
Third, we submitted the complete structured article and a fixed extraction prompt to the DeepSeek API in a single call. We used the deepseek-chat model with temperature set to 0 and a maximum output length of 8,192 tokens. The model generated one A claim, exactly one claim for every predefined B node, and no more than one paragraph-anchored C proposition for each paragraph that contained an argument. For each C node, the model returned only its paragraph anchor and normalized claim text; it did not select a source sentence span. The script then assigned the C node to the deepest A or B span containing that paragraph, ensuring that parent assignment followed the predefined document structure rather than a separate model judgment. For auditability, the exact prompt and the unprocessed model response were saved alongside the structured output. This whole-document call replaced an earlier multi-call procedure that separately compressed and processed paragraphs.
Fourth, we normalized the response into a canonical tree. Node identifiers, node types, parent identifiers, paragraph provenance, claim text, and source fields were represented in a fixed JSON schema. The tree was also exported in two derived forms: a logical-tree file containing only A, B, and C nodes, with all M identifiers removed, and an encoded-tree file retaining the fixed M identifiers for calculation. The M layer was not regenerated by the model: it came directly from the source database constructed in Steps 2 and 3. Separating these representations prevented visible source codes from influencing subsequent semantic comparison while preserving exact evidence identities for edge-based calculation.
This encoding constituted the direct computational basis of CCI and ECI. For CCI, the normalized claims represented by B and C nodes in the generated-paper tree were aligned with semantically corresponding B and C claims in the human-authored reference tree; the comparison therefore operated from claim unit to claim unit, not from sentence to sentence or paragraph to paragraph. For ECI, an edge was defined as either a direct parent-child relation between two B/C claim units or a relation connecting a B/C claim unit to a fixed M source identifier. ECI consequently measured whether the generated paper reproduced both the organization of claims and the links between claims and historical evidence. The logical tree without M was used for semantic alignment so that visible source identifiers could not influence claim matching, whereas the encoded tree was used afterward to calculate source-related edges through exact M-identifier correspondence. Because every aggregate value could be decomposed into a list of matched and unmatched nodes and edges, the calculation retained an inspectable audit trail from the final score back to the underlying claims and sources.
Fifth, we bound and reviewed the source relations. The script attached to each C node the fixed M identifiers recorded in that C node's anchor paragraph; the extraction model did not create or rename these identifiers. Each B node then inherited the union of the M identifiers attached to its descendant C nodes, and A was associated with the article's complete, fixed source roster. Reviewers checked whether each extracted claim was faithful to its paragraph, whether paragraph-based source binding was substantively appropriate, and whether the predefined structural span placed the claim under the correct parent. An automated validator checked identifier uniqueness, the existence and type of every parent, the one-A constraint, the agreement between source identifiers and the article's source roster, and the absence of broken branches. Passing this validator established structural consistency, not the historical truth of the extracted interpretation; semantic decisions remained subject to manual review.
Sixth, we repeated the complete extraction twice for each paper using the same model, prompt, input, and sampling settings. The two runs were retained as extraction replicates for inspecting output stability; the number of runs was fixed in advance and was not increased in response to the observed agreement. Each run was stored separately and included the structured input, exact prompt, raw response, canonical JSON tree, logical tree without M, encoded tree with M, model and parameter settings, and timestamp. The extraction script wrote new output files by default and required an explicit overwrite option to replace an existing run; when overwrite was used, it first created a backup. API credentials were excluded from the archived materials.
In the subsequent paper-generation stage, the same six-step procedure will be applied to the LLM-generated papers. The generated paper, rather than a model-produced outline, will serve as the input to the extractor. Human-authored and generated papers will therefore be converted into the same observable representation using the same extraction model and rules. The article-level conclusion A will be retained in the archived tree but excluded from the claim- and edge-convergence denominators because each paper necessarily contains a single top-level conclusion. The neutral research question Q will be stored as an experimental control and will not be treated as a claim node in the argument tree.
5. Paper Generation and Processing
For each human-authored source article, we will cross two prompt conditions with three source-context conditions. In the neutral-question condition (Q), the model will receive the research question and source pool; in the argument-guided condition (A), it will additionally receive a predefined central proposition. The source materials will be supplied at the short (S), medium (M), or long (L) context level described in Step 3. The six conditions (QS, QM, QL, AS, AM, and AL) will each be run with GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-5.6 Sol. Three separate generations will be obtained for every article-by-model-by-condition combination, giving 36 generated papers per source article and a planned total of 1,080 papers across the 30 source articles. The repetition count will be fixed before formal generation begins.
Reasoning effort (the interface's thinking level) will be a controlled variable, fixed at medium for both models and every generation condition. It will be recorded separately from the S/M/L source-context setting. The same named effort setting does not establish equal computational expenditure across models; the comparison therefore concerns the two models under a common interface setting. Prompt wording, material order within each context pool, output language, length requirements, source-identifier rules, tool permissions, and researcher intervention will also be held constant. Within each article and source-context level, Q and A prompts will differ only in the central-proposition instruction. The two models and the medium setting must be available on both accounts before formal generation; a change in model or effort setting will require a documented protocol amendment.
Generation will be conducted manually through Codex using the team's two ChatGPT Plus accounts. Each paper will be generated in a fresh task, with no preceding experimental conversation, using a frozen prompt and the designated source pool. Both operators will use the same global instructions, skill and plugin configuration, and workspace access rules. Each task will have access only to its designated input materials; access to original article prose, reference argument trees, prior outputs, and analysis reports will be excluded. Browsing and subagent delegation will be disabled for generation. These measures reduce contextual interference but do not remove Codex's system instructions or guarantee an identical hidden backend environment. For each run, we will archive the complete prompt, input hashes, raw output, displayed model identifier, reasoning-effort setting, execution time, anonymized account identifier, and exposed generation settings. Settings that the interface does not expose will be recorded as unavailable.
Runs will follow a prespecified schedule that interleaves the two models and the six conditions across accounts and execution dates. Both accounts will execute Q and A prompts at all three context levels for both models. Within each article, model, and condition, account assignment will alternate across the three repeats; the account receiving the third repeat will be counterbalanced across articles. A feasibility pilot covering one article from each thematic stratum will test input isolation, output capture, and the workload of downstream extraction and alignment before formal generation. If used to revise the protocol, pilot outputs will be archived separately and excluded from the formal sample. Protocol revisions will be finalized before examining formal CCI/ECI differences. Model unavailability or a displayed version change during collection will be logged and addressed through a protocol amendment rather than an unrecorded substitution.
Generated papers will be required to retain the source identifiers established in Step 3. Before argument-tree extraction, scripts will check output completeness, source-identifier validity, compliance with the prespecified length rule, and the presence of archival files. Researchers will not revise, extend, or polish the generated prose. Empty responses, explicit interface errors, or visibly interrupted transmission may receive at most one replacement attempt in a new task; both attempts and the replacement reason will be retained. Weak argumentation, disagreement with the original article, and invalid source citations will be recorded as output characteristics and will not trigger a replacement. Persistent technical failures and outputs that cannot be processed will remain in the run ledger and be reported by condition. Each complete generated paper will undergo the same extraction, normalization, source-binding, and validation procedure as the human-authored papers in Step 4, using a common extraction model and common evaluation rules across both generation models.
6. Data Analysis and Statistical Evaluation
During the preliminary stages of the experiment, we explored two computational approaches for evaluating the similarity between argument structures: the multilingual natural language inference model mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli-multilingual-nli-2mil7 and cosine similarity based on text embeddings. However, our preliminary results, consistent with findings reported in related research [M, O], indicated that these approaches had limitations in evaluating complex propositions in ways that reliably aligned with human judgments. In particular, complex historical claims lack a ready-made gold standard for assessing semantic and relational equivalence. Consequently, we adopted an LLM-based evaluation procedure under fixed experimental conditions.
For each comparison, the model evaluated anonymized B/C claim units from the generated-paper tree against those from the human-authored reference tree. In each round, it identified pairs that were semantically equivalent or stood in a one-way entailment relation. The left-right order and temporary identifiers were randomized so that the judgment did not depend on the original node codes or a fixed candidate position. Each comparison was evaluated in ten independent rounds. A claim pair was accepted in the main analysis when it was identified in at least six rounds; a threshold of four rounds was retained for robustness analysis. The claim convergence index (CCI) was then calculated as the number of eligible B/C claims in the human reference tree with at least one accepted match divided by the total number of eligible B/C claims in that tree. The analysis files additionally retained the corresponding model-side precision, allowing unmatched claims introduced by the generated paper to be inspected without changing the definition of the primary CCI measure. Edge convergence was calculated only after this voted claim mapping had been fixed: an original claim-to-claim or claim-to-source edge counted as preserved when the mapped generated claims reproduced the same direct relation or shared the same fixed M identifier. Claim convergence was reported as CCI and edge convergence as ECI throughout the analysis code, intermediate data, and final reports.
For the planned two-model comparison, each source article will remain the primary unit of inference. The three generation repeats will characterize within-model output variability and will not be treated as independent source articles. Q-versus-A and source-context contrasts will be evaluated within articles, with generation model included as an experimental factor; model-specific contrasts and model-by-condition interactions will assess whether the direction and magnitude of these effects are consistent across GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-5.6 Sol. Reasoning effort will not be estimated as a treatment effect because it is fixed at medium. Results will be interpreted as conditional on this effort setting, the two selected models, and the observed Codex environment. Failure rates and reasons for unavailable scores will be reported alongside the convergence results.
